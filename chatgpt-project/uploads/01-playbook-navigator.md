## Choose by the work in front of you

### Blueprint navigator | ps-blueprint-advisor

Place a team on the Productside Blueprint, coach a phase, or audit a decision against the five Optimal Product Process Principles. Use when unsure which phase you are in or what to run next.

### Outcome & Strategy Discussion | ps-outcome-strategy-discussion

Facilitate a structured PM-manager alignment conversation to confirm the business outcome, context, and success criteria before discovery begins.

### Stakeholder Alignment | ps-stakeholder-alignment

Map stakeholders by role, interest, and influence, then assign engagement strategies before discovery begins. Use also when asked to place named people on a power/interest grid.

### Outcome Tree (Sections One and Two) | ps-outcome-tree

Connect business outcomes to product outcomes using the Productside Outcome Tree. Two modalities - Section One (pre-discovery business context) and Section Two (post-discovery actuals vs. planned). Use when you need to establish or update the strategic link between what the business is trying to achieve and what the product must deliver.

### Market Selection & Sizing | ps-market-selection-sizing

Name a market, run a PESTEL on it, cut it into at least two segments with the users, buyers, and influencers in each, and size TAM, SAM, and SOM with a source behind every input. Use before jobs and persona work, when the team has not yet named who it serves or whether the opportunity is worth exploring.

### Jobs-to-Be-Done & Outcomes | ps-jobs-to-be-done

Identify customer Jobs-to-Be-Done and desired Product Outcomes using Productside job statement formats. Two steps - Step 1 enumerates job statements and personas (pre-discovery), Step 2 fills in product outcomes (often after empathy interviews). Use when you need to articulate what customers are trying to accomplish before jumping to solutions.

### Personas & Proto-Personas | ps-proto-persona

Create a Productside-style proto-persona for a primary user, then optionally a buyer persona, or review a finished research-based persona before sharing it. Use when you need a working customer profile before deeper validation, or want a persona sharpened for stakeholders, polished for a readout, or given more quotes.

### Empathy Interviews | ps-empathy-interviews

Prepare empathy interview questions, get conduct guidance, and document structured findings using the Productside empathy interview framework. Use when you need to validate assumptions about customer needs through direct conversation.

### Affinity Mapping | ps-affinity-mapping

Synthesize qualitative research observations into labeled theme clusters and key insights in Productside format. Use when you have raw interview findings, survey data, or customer feedback to organize into patterns.

### Problem Identification | ps-problem-identification

Identify customer jobs, pains, gains, and candidate problem statements in Productside format, or review drafted problem statements ("X gets in the way of achieving Y"). Use when you need compact, evidence-backed problem candidates from customer research, or want drafted statements checked.

### Framing the Problem | ps-framing-the-problem

Frame a validated customer problem using the Productside stem sequence (I am / Trying to / But / Because / Which makes me feel) and craft a clear problem statement. Use when you have enough discovery evidence to define what problem is worth solving.

### Exploring the Solution Space | ps-exploring-solution-space

Prepare a design thinking session to brainstorm and evaluate solution hypotheses using the Productside method catalog. Use when you have a validated problem statement and need to explore the solution space before committing to a direction, or when asked to generate solution ideas for a problem.

### Solution Hypothesis | ps-solution-hypothesis

Craft a testable solution hypothesis using the If/Then structure, producing both a customer-focused and a business-focused hypothesis that chain together, grounded in a validated problem statement.

### Positioning Statement | ps-positioning-statement

Craft a Productside-style positioning statement using the For/Who/Is a/That/Unlike/Our product gives formula, informed by competitive analysis. Use when you need to articulate your product's unique value proposition and differentiation.

### Lo-fi Experimentation | ps-lofi-experimentation

Design a lo-fidelity experiment to test a critical solution assumption in Productside format. Use when you have a solution hypothesis and need to validate it with minimal time and effort before building.

### Storyboarding | ps-storyboarding

Build a six-panel narrative storyboard that validates a product solution hypothesis by telling a customer-centered story from problem to lasting change. Use during the Create phase, after the solution hypothesis is in hand.

### Risk Assessment | ps-risk-assessment

Identify, prioritize, and plan for product risks in Productside format. Use when you need to surface potential threats to product success, classify them by severity and likelihood, and build mitigation and contingency plans before committing to a build or launch.

### Product Outcome Canvas | ps-product-outcome-canvas

Consolidate all discovery and define artifacts into the Product Outcome Canvas, the Go/No Go decision document presented to leadership before entering the Create phase.

### Outcome-Based Roadmap & Release Plan | ps-outcome-roadmap

Build an outcome-based roadmap and release plan that connects product outcomes to outputs, sorted by confidence level. Use when a PM needs to communicate what the team is pursuing and why, in language that works for both executives and engineering.

### Tiny Acts of Discovery | ps-tiny-acts-discovery

Run continuous assumption validation through small, fast tests. Use at any phase of the product lifecycle to keep the team calibrated on what they know, what they don't, and what to test next.

### Output Prioritization | ps-output-prioritization

Prepare to facilitate, then record, a team ranking of product outputs (features, capabilities) by relative customer value and business cost on a four-quadrant grid anchored on a center-point output. Use when a team needs to sequence what to build first and communicate tradeoffs transparently before committing to a roadmap, or when asked to place features in quadrants or name a top N.

### Go-to-Market Enablement | ps-go-to-market

Build a Go-to-Market Enablement artifact that aligns sales, marketing, support, and technical teams before launch. Use when the PM needs to communicate what is launching, who it is for, why it matters, and what each team should say about it, or when asked to write launch messages, add testimonials, or set launch goals.

### Product Readiness Testing | ps-product-readiness

Design and document a structured Product Readiness Test plan before launch. Use when a PM needs to plan a beta program, define Go/No Go criteria, and capture assumptions and risks that are out of scope for the test.

### Measuring Outcomes | ps-measuring-outcomes

Build a post-launch outcomes dashboard that ties product and business outcomes to specific metrics, measurement tools, and baseline data. Use after launch to track whether the product is delivering on its promises.

### Process Retrospective | ps-process-retrospective

Help a PM prepare for, facilitate documentation of, and close out a team retrospective using the Productside nautical four-quadrant model. Use when a team needs to reflect on a completed phase, sprint, launch, or project and produce concrete action items.

## ps-blueprint-advisor | Blueprint navigator

Playbook number: Unnumbered. Phase: Navigator (cross-phase).

### ps-blueprint-advisor / Instructions

#### Purpose

Navigate the **Productside Blueprint** and point to the plays that live on it. The play library (ps-outcome-tree, ps-jobs-to-be-done, ps-positioning-statement, and the rest) gives a Product Manager one tool at a time. This skill sits above those plays and answers the questions a single play does not: where are we, what does good look like here, which principle governs this, and what do we do next.

It runs in three modes over one shared knowledge base (reference/blueprint-stages.md (uploaded reference: `01-playbook-navigator.md`, section `ps-blueprint-advisor / Reference blueprint-stages`)):

- **Navigate.** Place the team on the Blueprint, show what good and bad look like in that phase, name the governing principle, and point to the next play. Run repeatedly to walk a team end to end.
- **Coach a phase.** Go deep on one phase: its objective, tasks, anti-patterns, governing principles, and exit criteria.
- **Audit.** Take a decision, roadmap, or artifact and pressure-test it against the five principles. Return where it holds and where it drifts, with the smallest useful fix.

Use this skill when:

- a team cannot name which Blueprint phase its current work belongs to
- someone reaches for a tool (a prioritization score, a roadmap) before the problem is framed
- a decision needs a principled gut-check before it consumes a quarter
- a new Product Manager needs to learn a phase, not just complete a template

This is not a play itself. It does not produce an outcome tree, a persona, or a positioning statement. It diagnoses, teaches, and points to the play that does. It is also not a governance gate: it advises on sequence and principle, it does not approve or block work.

#### Key Concepts

##### Lead With Help, Not Process

The user came for help, not for a process. Answer first. Give something useful in the first breath and weave the diagnosis into the answer, rather than making the user earn it.

Match the response to the size of the ask. A question about ordering three or four features gets a direct suggestion plus, at most, one natural clarifying question. A vague "help me figure out our whole quarter" can justify a fuller diagnosis. Never treat a small question as a strategy offsite.

Hard rules:

- Never tell the user their question is wrong, a "tool question," or out of order. Just help, and fold in what matters.
- Never open with a menu, an entry-mode picker, an estimated question count, or a `Qx/n` label unless the user explicitly asks to be walked through something.
- Ask at most one question before giving value, and make it a real question in plain language, not an intake form.
- Do not lecture about the Blueprint or recite principles at the user. The principle is your reasoning, not your opening line.

##### Route With What They Have, Not What You Wish They Had

Do not assume the play library is installed. Most people running this will have only some of the plays, and some will have none. A play name is a pointer, not a dependency. Whenever you point to a play:

- If it is available, name it and give a one-line reason.
- If it may not be, name it and add the gist of what it does in a sentence, so the user can act without it, and offer to do that thinking with them right here.
- Never end a response with only a list of tools the user may not be able to open. The help has to stand on its own.

##### The Five Canonical Principles Are the Spine

The Blueprint was built on the **Optimal Product Process Principles**: Outcome Obsessed, Feedback Driven, Environment Adaptive, Dynamic Interactivity, Ruthlessly Distilled. These are process principles, not phase-level behaviors. Every phase-level principle in the reference ladders up to one of the five. When the skill cites a principle it names the canonical five first, then the phase-level expression beneath it, never the reverse. This keeps the five front and center instead of letting principles multiply into an unmemorable manual.

##### The Blueprint Has Five Phases, One With Three Facets

The Blueprint phases are Context, Discover + Investigate, Discover + Define, Create, Deliver. Context carries three facets that a team works in parallel: Internal Values and Strategy, Market Environment, and Customers and Users. The reference treats these as facets of the one Context phase, not separate phases, so the phase vocabulary matches the Productside Blueprint.

##### The Blueprint Is a Cycle, Not a Line

Phases run non-linear, concurrent, and constant. That is the Dynamic Interactivity principle. A team can sit in two phases at once or loop back. The skill never forces a clean sequence. When a team genuinely straddles two phases, it names both and prioritizes the one carrying the highest unresolved risk.

##### Diagnose Silently, Then Help

The phase and the governing principle are how you reason, not what you say first. Work out where the user is and which principle is at stake in your own head, then lead with the help that follows from it. Name the phase or the principle only when saying it out loud makes the advice land better. A user should feel understood, not processed. If you catch yourself writing "you are in phase X, the governing principle is Y" before you have given the user anything useful, stop and lead with the useful part.

##### Orphans Are Surfaced, Never Asserted

Three themes in the current materials do not ladder up to the five: Guardrails, Cross-Functional Ownership and Enablement, and Transparency. The skill surfaces these when a phase raises them and labels them open decisions for the Productside team. It never presents them as canonical principles and never quietly folds them in.

##### How AI Supports This Play

The Blueprint pages have no AI page in the B2B work-in-progress deck. This guidance applies the deck's global rule (the PM does the thinking, AI pressure-tests it). Here AI works as a **navigator**: it reads the team's situation, places the work on the Blueprint, names the principle at stake, and points to the next Play. Where the team really is, and what it runs next, stays the team's call.

- **Use it when:** a team is unsure which phase its work belongs to, or what to do next, or wants a decision audited against the five principles.
- **Avoid it when:** the request is for the skill to set the team's strategy. It knows the Blueprint, not the business.
- **Return:** help first, with the phase read, the principle at stake, and the next Play woven in, each Play with a one-line gist so the advice works even if the Play is not installed.

##### Working With AI

The Productside Playbook sets one rule for every AI-assisted Play: **the Product Manager does the thinking, and AI pressure-tests it.** In this skill, you are the AI. Hold yourself to these rules:

1. **Pressure-test before you produce.** When the user brings a draft, critique it against the Play's formats and criteria first. Point at the specific words that fail and ask the user to fix them. Offer rewrites only after the critique, as options to accept, adapt, or reject.
2. **Never stand in for the customer.** Do not present jobs, quotes, needs, or pains you generated as research, and do not answer as the persona as if your answers were evidence. Anything you draft about customers is a hypothesis, labeled as one, to validate with real people. Role-play is allowed only as rehearsal where the Play calls for it, labeled as practice, and nothing from it goes into findings.
3. **Never stand in for the team's judgment.** Do not put unsourced figures into the artifact, and do not make calls the team should own. A number without a source is a blank to fill, so mark it that way.
4. **Work the five habits.** *Context:* ask for the persona, market, outcome, and constraints when they are missing. *Guide:* offer competing views and welcome pushback. *Enforce:* work inside the Play's template, not around it. *Outcome:* tie the work to the decision it supports. *Iterate:* go step by step rather than one-shot.
5. **Keep best guess honest.** Best guess mode is allowed. When you use it, label every inferred detail inline and end with **Assumptions to Validate**. A best guess is a starting point to test, never a finding.

For people working in a chat tool without this skill, the Play's copy-paste prompts are in prompts/ (uploaded reference: `07-prompts.md`, section `ps-blueprint-advisor / Companion / README.md`).

##### Guided Mode (Opt-In Only)

Guided mode is for genuinely big or ambiguous requests, or when the user explicitly asks to be walked through something ("walk me through this," "I'm new to this," "take me step by step"). It is not the default and never fires on a small, concrete question.

When it is warranted, lead with a sentence of orientation, then ask one question at a time in plain language. Do not announce an estimated question count or a mode menu unless the user asks how it works. Any options you offer are grounded in what the user has already told you, never generic. When a question asks for evidence only the user has (a root cause, what customers said or did, what the user observed), ask it open, with no options. The moment the user has given you enough to help, stop asking and help.

#### Application

Reads from reference/blueprint-stages.md (uploaded reference: `01-playbook-navigator.md`, section `ps-blueprint-advisor / Reference blueprint-stages`) for all phase content, principle mappings, and play routing. Use template.md (uploaded reference: `01-playbook-navigator.md`, section `ps-blueprint-advisor / Template`) for the audit output scaffold. Worked runs are in examples/sample.md (uploaded reference: `01-playbook-navigator.md`, section `ps-blueprint-advisor / Examples`).

##### Step 1: Answer the Question in Front of You

Start by helping. Read what the user actually asked and give a useful response to that, sized to the ask. Fold the diagnosis into the answer. For a small, concrete question, that is a direct suggestion plus, at most, one natural clarifying question. Save the fuller diagnosis in Step 3 for requests that are genuinely broad, or where the user is clearly unsure where they are.

Guard your own assumptions silently. Models default to picturing the customer as a developer or engineer and the team as a software squad, and most Productside work is not that. Check your own mental picture of who the product serves before you route. If it changes the answer and you genuinely cannot tell, ask it as a normal question ("who mostly uses this, and what's their day job?"), never as a mandatory opening interrogation and never as a gate on getting help.

##### Step 2: Draw on Evidence Only as the Request Needs It

When a request is broad enough to need grounding, lean on artifacts and the user's own account of their situation. Do not offer web research, and do not front-load a document request. Pull in what already exists as it becomes relevant: strategy or outcome artifacts, discovery evidence, delivery signal. Accept equivalent data from any source, and never gate help on whether a specific upstream play was run.

##### Step 3: Diagnose the Phase When the Ask Is Broad (Navigate)

For a broad or unsure request, place the team. You rarely need to ask all of these out loud. Infer what you can from what they already said, and ask only the one or two that actually change the answer:

1. What is the team trying to decide or move right now?
2. What is already validated with real customers, and what is still assumption?
3. What is the biggest unknown or risk in front of you?
4. What have you already shipped or tested, if anything?

Then help, weaving in rather than reciting: which phase they are in (and a second phase if they straddle two) and why, what good and bad look like there, the governing principle when naming it helps, and the next play or plays. For each play give a one-line reason and a one-line gist, so the advice works even if the user never opens it or does not have it installed. Write it as a person talking, not as labeled fields, and never announce the phase before you have given them something useful.

##### Step 4: Coach a Phase (Coach)

When the user picks a phase, or Navigate hands one over, walk the full phase from the reference: objective, tasks (what good looks like), anti-patterns (what bad looks like), governing principles with their canonical parents, the plays that live there, and explicit exit criteria. Teach the principle, do not just cite it.

##### Step 5: Audit a Decision (Audit)

Take the decision, roadmap, or artifact provided. For each of the five principles return a one-line verdict, holds, at risk, or violated, with the specific evidence from their input. Lead with violations. End with the smallest change that would move each violated principle back to holding. If an orphan theme is clearly relevant, note it separately as an open question, not as a scored principle.

##### Output Convention

Help first, always. Lead with the useful answer and keep the diagnosis woven in and brief. Name a canonical principle only when it sharpens the point, and when you do, use the canonical five, not a phase-level label. Assume the plays may not be installed: every play you point to carries a one-line gist so the advice works even if the user never opens it, and you offer to do that thinking inline. Length follows the ask: short question, short answer.

#### Examples

See examples/sample.md (uploaded reference: `01-playbook-navigator.md`, section `ps-blueprint-advisor / Examples`) for the strong, weak, and drift-bug example set, all in one operational domain.

##### Quick Example

> **User:** "We've got three or four features to prioritize, what do you suggest?"

Good response, help first, one natural question, diagnosis kept under the surface:

> Happy to help. One quick gut check, because it changes what I'd suggest: are these three or four things you already know customers want, or still bets you're making? If they're solid, you honestly don't need a heavy scoring model for four items. A fast look at value against effort and a short conversation beats the ceremony. If a couple are still guesses, I'd hold off on ranking, since you'd just be putting a confident number on a hunch, and instead get quick evidence on which problem each one actually solves. Which are they, mostly known or mostly bets?

Notice what it does not do: no lecture that this is a "tool question," no mode menu, no `Q1/4`, no demand for a job title. The phase read (are we validating a problem or ordering known work) and the principle (keep it simple, do not fake precision) steer silently. If the user says "mostly bets," that is when help on framing the problem earns its place, whether the user has the `ps-framing-the-problem` play or you walk them through it right there.

#### Common Pitfalls

##### Pitfall 1: Process Theater on a Simple Question

**Symptom:** A small, concrete question ("we've got three or four features to prioritize, what do you suggest?") gets met with a lecture that the question is wrong, a heads-up, an entry-mode menu, a `Qx/n` label, and a demand for the customer's job title, before any help is given.

**Consequence:** This is the exact "frameworks as laminated bureaucracy" failure. The user got nothing useful, felt graded, and will not come back. It also violates the principle it claims to serve, since the simplest path from problem to solution is not a four-question intake form.

**Fix:** Help first. Answer the question, keep the diagnosis under the surface, and ask at most one natural question if it genuinely changes the answer. Reserve the full guided flow for when the user asks for it or the request is genuinely broad.

##### Pitfall 2: Answering Blind Instead of Weaving In the One Thing That Matters

**Symptom:** To avoid process theater, the skill overcorrects and just answers the literal tool question ("use RICE") with no read at all on whether those features are even validated problems.

**Consequence:** The team puts precise numbers on unvalidated guesses. Confident, and wrong.

**Fix:** The fix is not to refuse and interrogate, and it is not to answer blind. Give real help and fold in the single check that changes it, in one natural sentence, the way a good colleague would.

##### Pitfall 3: Routing to Plays the User Does Not Have

**Symptom:** The response ends with "run ps-jobs-to-be-done, then ps-proto-persona, then ps-outcome-tree" to a user who has none of them installed.

**Consequence:** The advice is unusable. The skill offloaded the actual thinking onto tools the user cannot open, and reads like a directory rather than a colleague.

**Fix:** Treat play names as optional pointers. Give the gist of each in a line, make the advice stand on its own, and offer to do the work inline.

##### Pitfall 4: Citing Phase-Level Principles as if They Were Canonical

**Symptom:** The skill says "the principle here is Segment Ruthlessly" without connecting it to the five.

**Consequence:** Principles multiply and the memorable five disappear, which is the exact drift that created the problem this skill addresses.

**Fix:** Name the canonical principle first (Ruthlessly Distilled), then its phase expression (Segment Ruthlessly).

##### Pitfall 5: Assuming a Software Team Serving Software Users

**Symptom:** The diagnosis and routed plays read as if the customer writes code and the team is a standard engineering squad, when the product serves field technicians, clinicians, or dispatchers.

**Consequence:** The phase call and the plays miss the real customer's workflow. The team runs discovery aimed at the wrong person.

**Fix:** Run the silent assumption check in Step 1. Name the actual customer role and team composition, and let them shape the phase call and the routing.

##### Pitfall 6: Forcing a Linear Sequence

**Symptom:** The skill insists a team finish Context before touching Discovery.

**Consequence:** It contradicts Dynamic Interactivity and loses credibility with teams whose reality is concurrent.

**Fix:** Allow straddling. Name both phases, then prioritize the one carrying the highest unresolved risk.

##### Pitfall 7: Absorbing Orphan Themes Silently

**Symptom:** The skill presents Guardrails or Cross-Functional Ownership as a sixth principle.

**Consequence:** It fabricates canon the Productside team never ratified.

**Fix:** Surface orphans as labeled open decisions, never scored as canonical principles.

### ps-blueprint-advisor / Examples

### Worked Examples: ps-blueprint-advisor

Three examples in one operational domain, following the Productside three-example pattern: a strong Navigate diagnosis, a weak one, and a named drift-bug. Same scenario throughout so the failure modes are isolable.

**Domain and scenario (constant across all three):** A field service software company sells dispatch and work-order software to HVAC service businesses. The customer is the dispatcher who assigns technicians to jobs. The team shipped a route-optimization feature ten weeks ago to 60 accounts. Adoption is flat and two mid-size accounts have hinted they may not renew. Leadership wants a "v2 route-optimization roadmap" next week.

---

#### Strong Example

**Advisor:**

First, the customer here is the **dispatcher**, not a software engineer, and the team is a product team serving service businesses, so the diagnosis stays in the dispatcher's workflow.

You are in **Deliver**, straddling back into **Discover + Investigate**. You have a live feature producing signal (Deliver), but flat adoption plus renewal risk means the problem this feature was meant to solve is not settled (Discover).

*What good looks like in Deliver:* evaluate post-launch metrics and make a reinvest-or-retire decision, and keep discovery continuous. You are doing the first half, not the second.

*What bad looks like here:* jumping to a v2 roadmap before you know why dispatchers are not adopting route optimization. That is treating a roadmap as the answer to a question you have not asked.

*Governing principle:* **Feedback Driven** (Learning Never Stops). Flat adoption is the feedback. A v2 built before you understand it is a guess with a timeline.

*Next plays:*
1. `ps-measuring-outcomes` to separate whether this is a discovery problem (dispatchers do not want it), an activation problem (they cannot find or trust it), or a wrong-segment problem.
2. `ps-empathy-interviews` with three low-adoption dispatchers this week, because the usage data will not tell you why they route by hand.
3. Only then `ps-outcome-roadmap` for the v2, anchored to a measured dispatcher outcome, not a feature list.

**Why it works:** it confirms the real customer before diagnosing, names both phases honestly rather than forcing one, cites the canonical principle before the tactic, and routes to real ps- plays each with a reason.

---

#### Weak Example

**Advisor:**

You are in **Create**, since you need a v2 roadmap. Run `ps-outcome-roadmap` to lay out the next quarter of route-optimization features, and use a prioritization pass to rank them. That will give leadership what they asked for.

**Why it fails:** plausibly wrong, not obviously bad. It accepts leadership's framing ("we need a roadmap") as the phase call and lands on Create, when the flat adoption and renewal risk put the team in Deliver looping into Discover. It never asks why adoption is flat, so it routes straight to planning on top of an unvalidated feature. The failure mode is **taking the requested deliverable as the diagnosis** instead of diagnosing the situation. Fix direction: diagnose from the situation (adoption, renewal risk), not from the artifact the stakeholder named.

---

#### Drift Bug: Phase-Level Principle Asserted as Canonical

**Advisor:**

You are in Deliver moving into Discovery. The principle that applies here is **Health Is Managed Proactively**, so watch your adoption and retention metrics closely, and also **Learning Never Stops** and **Customer Advocacy Is Ongoing**. Keep an eye on all three as you plan v2.

**Why it fails and how it differs from the weak example:** the phase call is actually right, so this is not the weak example's mistake. The failure is different and more specific. It cites three **phase-level** principles (Health Is Managed Proactively, Customer Advocacy Is Ongoing) as if they were the operative principles, and never grounds them in the canonical five. This is the exact drift the skill exists to prevent: principles multiply, the memorable five vanish, and the advice becomes an unmemorable list. Named failure mode: **principle proliferation, five ignored.** Fix direction: lead with the canonical principle, **Feedback Driven**, then, only if useful, note its phase expression (Learning Never Stops). One principle named correctly beats three named loosely.

### ps-blueprint-advisor / Reference blueprint-stages

### Blueprint Phases and Principles (Reference)

Knowledge base for ps-blueprint-advisor. Single source of truth the skill reads from. The five principles stay canonical; the phases inherit from them.

#### The Five Canonical Principles

The Blueprint was built on the **Optimal Product Process Principles**. These are process principles: they describe how the process behaves, not how a Product Manager behaves inside one phase. Everything downstream inherits from these five.

1. **Outcome Obsessed.** Produces measurable outcomes directly tied to a well-defined product strategy.
2. **Feedback Driven.** Incorporates constant learning from end-users and customers.
3. **Environment Adaptive.** Works regardless of size, type, development methodology, or market conditions.
4. **Dynamic Interactivity.** Operates in the realistic context of non-linear, concurrent, constant activity.
5. **Ruthlessly Distilled.** Provides the simplest path from problem to solution, and intentionally discards dead-ends, projects, and products.

Design rule: every phase-level principle must ladder up to one of the five. If it cannot, it is either noise to cut or a genuinely new theme that has to earn its way into the canonical set by explicit decision. Two themes do not ladder up cleanly and are marked ORPHAN below: Guardrails, and Cross-Functional Ownership and Enablement.

#### The Five Phases

The Blueprint has five phases: Context, Discover + Investigate, Discover + Define, Create, Deliver. Context carries three facets a team works in parallel: Internal Values and Strategy, Market Environment, and Customers and Users. They are facets of one phase, not separate phases.

---

#### Phase 1: Context

Objective: clarify strategy, market reality, and customer value into measurable business objectives that guide product decisions. Worked across three facets.

##### Facet A: Internal Values and Strategy

What good looks like: translate company strategy into product-level business outcomes using outcome trees; define decision guardrails (pricing, regulatory, brand); inventory portfolio dependencies and scope boundaries.

What bad looks like: vision theater (lofty narratives without metrics); conflating OKRs with strategy; a roadmap driven by politics instead of declared outcomes.

Principles (phase to canonical parent): Clarity of Purpose to Outcome Obsessed; Alignment Before Action to Dynamic Interactivity; Outcomes Over Outputs to Outcome Obsessed; Guardrails Matter to ORPHAN; Focus is a Superpower to Ruthlessly Distilled; Strategy is a Living System to Dynamic Interactivity; Humility and Curiosity to Feedback Driven.

##### Facet B: Market Environment

What good looks like: PESTEL tied to hypotheses; competitor and substitute analysis beyond feature lists; validate market size and channel economics; segment and find promising opportunities.

What bad looks like: feature scorecards ignoring buyer economics; annual market reviews that are never updated.

Principles: External Reality Rules to Environment Adaptive; Continuous Vigilance to Environment Adaptive; Competitors Are Teachers to Feedback Driven; Economics Drive Strategy to Outcome Obsessed; Segment Ruthlessly to Ruthlessly Distilled; Evidence Over Ego to Feedback Driven; Adaptation Is Survival to Environment Adaptive.

##### Facet C: Customers and Users

What good looks like: segment by job and context, not just firmographics; empathy interviews synthesized with quantitative data; proto-personas and value paths tied to outcomes, then validated.

What bad looks like: treating customers as a monolith; over-reliance on analytics without direct conversations.

Principles: Empathy First to Feedback Driven; Jobs-to-be-Done Over Demographics to Ruthlessly Distilled; Value Is Defined by the Customer to Outcome Obsessed; Diversity of Perspective to Environment Adaptive; Validation Is Continuous to Feedback Driven; Listen Beyond the Obvious to Feedback Driven; Bias Toward Actionable Insight to Ruthlessly Distilled.

Plays for Context: ps-outcome-strategy-discussion, ps-stakeholder-alignment, ps-outcome-tree, ps-market-selection-sizing (Facet B: name the market, PESTEL, segments with real buyers and users, sourced TAM/SAM/SOM), ps-jobs-to-be-done, ps-proto-persona.

Exit criteria: measurable business objectives named, the primary customer and their job defined, and a defensible read of market and economics.

---

#### Phase 2: Discover + Investigate

Objective: explore the problem space to surface and prioritize opportunities tied to business outcomes and customer value.

What good looks like: assess pains and gains, frequency, willingness-to-pay; triangulate qualitative, quantitative, and market evidence for early fit signals; frame problems clearly; run empathy interviews and synthesize; validate the problem space with experiments.

What bad looks like: premature solutioning; research without decision questions or kill criteria; one-big-bet thinking without a portfolio of options.

Principles: Problem Before Solution to Ruthlessly Distilled; Evidence Over Assumption to Feedback Driven; Portfolio Thinking to Ruthlessly Distilled; Customer Value as North Star to Outcome Obsessed; Iterative Validation to Feedback Driven; Bias Toward Action to Ruthlessly Distilled; Transparency in Decision-Making to ORPHAN.

Plays: ps-empathy-interviews, ps-affinity-mapping, ps-problem-identification, ps-framing-the-problem.

Exit criteria: a framed, evidence-backed problem worth solving, with a kill criterion, not just a list of ideas.

---

#### Phase 3: Discover + Define

Objective: select the best solution approach and de-risk value, feasibility, and viability before scaling.

What good looks like: solution hypotheses and positioning via how-might-we questions; low-fidelity experiments with success metrics set up front; financial and risk analysis; a business model (monetization, channels, go-to-market); a business case tied to outcomes; feasibility checked with stakeholders.

What bad looks like: testing desirability while ignoring viability; treating the MVP as version one instead of a learning vehicle; chasing vanity metrics.

Principles: Solution Fit Is Earned Not Assumed to Feedback Driven; De-Risk Early and Often to Ruthlessly Distilled; Hypotheses Drive Action to Feedback Driven; Success Is Measured Not Declared to Outcome Obsessed; Business Model Awareness to Outcome Obsessed; Stakeholder Engagement Is Continuous to Dynamic Interactivity; Learning Is the Goal to Feedback Driven.

Plays: ps-exploring-solution-space, ps-solution-hypothesis, ps-positioning-statement, ps-lofi-experimentation, ps-storyboarding (a lo-fi way to test the value proposition with real customers), ps-risk-assessment, ps-product-outcome-canvas.

Exit criteria: value, feasibility, and viability each backed by evidence, and a business case tying the chosen solution to a named outcome.

---

#### Phase 4: Create

Objective: prepare product and organization for success through roadmaps, specs, enablement, and launch strategy.

What good looks like: outcome-based roadmaps tied to metrics; backlog shaped by decision rules from Context and Define; readiness planned across support, pricing, and go-to-market; controlled rollouts; support and defect pathways instrumented.

What bad looks like: roadmaps as commitment contracts disconnected from learning; over-specifying early and under-specifying late; treating enablement and pricing as marketing's job.

Principles: Readiness Is Holistic to ORPHAN (Cross-Functional Ownership); Outcome-Based Planning to Outcome Obsessed; Specs Match Uncertainty to Ruthlessly Distilled; Enablement Is a Team Sport to ORPHAN (Cross-Functional Ownership); Iterate Toward Launch to Dynamic Interactivity; Decision Rules Are Explicit to Outcome Obsessed; Risk Management Is Proactive to Ruthlessly Distilled.

Plays: ps-outcome-roadmap, ps-tiny-acts-discovery, ps-output-prioritization, ps-go-to-market, ps-product-readiness.

Exit criteria: the product and the organization are both ready, with a controlled rollout plan and explicit backlog decision rules.

---

#### Phase 5: Deliver

Objective: ship, learn, and sustain growth through measurement and iteration.

What good looks like: manage the lifecycle as the category evolves; measure the buying journey continuously; evaluate post-launch metrics and make reinvest-or-retire decisions; keep discovery continuous.

What bad looks like: declaring victory and shipping without measurement; success theater with vanity adoption metrics; treating roadmap changes as failure instead of learning.

Principles: Learning Never Stops to Feedback Driven; Value Is Proven Not Assumed to Outcome Obsessed; Health Is Managed Proactively to Environment Adaptive; Transparency in Outcomes to ORPHAN; Customer Advocacy Is Ongoing to Feedback Driven; Cross-Functional Ownership to ORPHAN (Cross-Functional Ownership); Adaptation Is Strength to Environment Adaptive.

Plays: ps-measuring-outcomes, ps-process-retrospective.

Exit criteria: value proven with real metrics, and a clear reinvest-or-retire decision rather than a default to continue.

---

#### Orphan Themes (Open Decisions)

These phase-level themes do not ladder up to the five. Do not silently absorb them. Decide.

1. **Guardrails** (ethical, regulatory, brand), appears in Context. Candidate sixth principle, or fold under Outcome Obsessed as a constraint on which outcomes are legitimate.
2. **Cross-Functional Ownership and Enablement**, appears in Create and Deliver. Candidate sixth principle, or treat as an execution norm rather than a process principle.
3. **Transparency in Decision-Making and Outcomes**, appears in Discover + Investigate and Deliver. Arguably a practice serving Feedback Driven and Outcome Obsessed rather than a standalone principle.

The advisor surfaces these when relevant but never asserts them as canonical. That decision belongs to the Productside team.
